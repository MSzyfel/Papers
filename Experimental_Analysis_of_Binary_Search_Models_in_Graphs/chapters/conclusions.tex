\chapter{Conclusions}

This thesis presented an experimental analysis of the generalized worst-case binary search problem in tree graphs. We explored various algorithms designed to tackle the Tree Search Problem with both uniform and non-uniform query costs. 

Obtaining efficient search strategies for trees is important due to numerous practical applications, such as bug searching, network diagnostics and river pollution detection. Additionally, the graph search problem is also important from a theoretical point of view. In particular, for the case of uniform costs, the depth od the decision tree corresponds to the well-known graph parameter called the \emph{tree-depth} \cite{GIANNOPOULOU20122089}. This parameter, tightly connected to other graph invariants like tree-width, is of wide interest in graph theory and parametrized complexity \cite{BODLAENDER1995238,NESETRIL20061022, BOROWIECKI2023113682}. The significance of the non-uniform cost model, on the other hand is because it is one of the few problems which are NP-hard already on trees. This is due to the fact that the decision trees have a non local structure, which in turn yields most of the classic dynamic programming techniques inapplicable. Approximation algorithm presented hereby, are therefore of particular interest, since they provide efficient ways of obtaining solutions of guaranteed quality for problems with such characteristics. It is interesting whether similar techniques can be applied to other, similar problems. Among them is for example the conflict-free connection coloring which differs only slightly since it requires that each path in the tree contains a vertex/edge with a unique color \cite{CfreeConOfTs}, a relaxed condition of the ranking coloring which is equivalent to the Tree Search Problem. 

The above mentioned non-locality enforces the use of complex algorithmic techniques, and non-polynomial time subroutines. This provides a two-fold challenge: on the one hand, it makes the algorithms hard to implement and debug, on the other hand, it limits their practical usability to relatively small instances. Due to this, the experimental analysis conducted in this thesis required significant effort. This war particularly demanding while implementing the QPTAS for $T||V, c||\COST_{max}$ both in terms of the used data structres (boxed decision trees) with operations on them (efficient merging and rotating) as well as the algorithm by itself, consisiting of few cases using complex subroutines. 

It is interesting, whether it is possible to derive similar results, using simpler techniques. In particular, a further research direction would be to further simplify the QPTAS both in terms of the large constant in the exponent and the algorithm itself. A central question in this line of research if whether is is possible to derive constant factor approximation guarantee for the non-uniform costs or ideally, a PTAS. As of right now, no inapproximability results are known for this problem, besides a trivial non-existence of absolute approximation algorithms. This problem therefore is included among the specific list of problems for which a $O\br{\sqrt{\log n}}$-approximation exists, but there is no lower bound matching this result. A similar issue is prevalent among many separation problems such as treewidth, vertex separators and edge cuts \cite{ExpFsGEandGPart,PartGsintoBComps, Improvedapproximationalgorithmsvertexseparators} for which $O\br{\sqrt{\log n}}$-approximation algorithms are known, but no non-trivial lower bounds beyond constant factor inapproximability are known. Interestingly, these problems are connected to Tree Search Problem, since they can be used to derive relatively simple constant factor approximation algorithms for the average case version of the problem \cite{szyfelbein2025approximatingaveragecasegraphsearch,OnDasHC,Acostfunctionforsimilaritybasedhierarchicalclustering}. 

The main conclusion drawn from the experimental analysis is that the state-of-the-art approximation algorithms algorithms are of practical use only for relatively small instances, up to several dozens of vertices. Beyond that, the $O\br{\log n/\log\log n}$-approximation algorithm is the most efficient one.  The $O\br{\log\log n}$-approximation algorithm, while theoretically interesting, is impractical for real-world applications due to its high computational complexity.

Additionally, on the random instances used in our experiments, the costs of the solutions built by all algorithms were relatively low, and no particular algorithm significantly outperformed the others. This suggests that on random instances, most heuristics perform well. However it should be noted that the least algorithmically complex procedure, i. e. the $O\br{\log n/\log\logn}$-approximation algorithm usually achieved slightly better quality then the others. This counterintuitive result suggests that on such instances, the most greedy-like algorithms perform best. This is visible in comparision to the $O\br{\log \log n}$ approximation algorithm achieving the worst approximation ratios. This is probably due to the fact that it processes many cost levels and each such level incurses additional $O\br{\OPT}$ cost. Therefore despite its better asymptotic guarantee, on it often performs slightly worse then the $O\br{\log n/\log\log n}$ and $O\br{\sqrt{\log n}}$-approximation algorithms.

Additionally, the approximation ratios observed in the experiments were significantly better than the theoretical worst-case guarantees, indicating that the algorithms are effective in practice despite their theoretical limitations. This observation is consistent with the general issue in contructing extremal instances for the approximation algorithms for the Tree Search Problem. We observe that such extremal instances are probably very rare in practice, as no sudden jumps in the approximation ratios were observed in the experiments. It should also be remarked that the approximation ratios obtained during the theoretical analysis are worst-case guarantees and in general, the intrinsic difficulty of achieving better results for the Tree Search Problem is lack of mathemathical techniques providing better lower bounds on the optimal solution. In particular the only lower bound used in the analysis of the QPTAS is the value of $1$ (after costs normalization) which is rather weak. Moreover during its recursive usage in the $O\br{\sqrt{\log n}}$-approximation algorithm, each such usage induces the cost of $O\br{\OPT}$, which is a lot considering the fact, that the size of the instances processed at each level of the recursion is significantly smaller then the original instance. Transcending any of these obstacles is a possible further research direction. 

A different way to decrease the approximation ratio would be to devise a dynamic program which stores less information for each box of the boxed decision tree. As of right now each of $\rank\br{T}$ boxes require enumerating $O\br{n}$ values of load. Reducing this amount to $\poly\br{\log n}$ would immediately yield a polynomial time algorithm with approximation ratio of $O\br{\log \log n}$. However, we think that this is a very challenging task, since the load values are crucial to ensure that the merging and rotating operations are performed correctly. A different way to achieve such approximation ratio would be to deisgn an algorithm similar to the one used for the $k$-up modular cost function which recursively splits the cost levels into $O\br{\log \log n}$ groups and processes each group separately. However, it is not clear how to perform such grouping in polynomial (or almost polynomial) time in a way which would ensure that the cost incurred at each level is $O\br{\OPT}$.

Another intersting observation is the suprisingly good performance of the $O\br{2^nn}$ time exact algorithm. Despite its exponential time complexity, it was able to solve instances with up to 33 vertices within reasonable time limits. In fact the upper bound on the running time of this algorithm is the number of connected subtrees of $T$. Since we generated our random trees using Prüfer sequences, by \cite{SubTsofRT} we know that the expected number of connected subtrees of a tree generated in this way is of order $O\br{1.4196^n}$. Although this is still exponential, it is significantly better than the worst-case bound of $O\br{2^n}$. This suggests that the exact algorithm may be practical for small to medium-sized instances, especially when the input trees have certain structural properties that limit the number of connected subtrees. In recent years many NP-hard problems, such as the Traveling Salesman Problem or the Steiner Tree Problem, have seen significant improvements in their exact exponential time algorithms \cite{ExpTAlgs}. It is therefore an interesting research direction to investigate whether similar improvements can be obtained for the Tree Search Problem.

Another advantage of the $O\br{\log n/\log \log n}$-approximation algorithm is the stability of its running time. No sudden running time spikes were observed for the procedure. In contrast, both of the procedures based on the QPTAS exhibited significant variance in their running times, even on instances of the same size. This is due to the fact that whenever the QPTAS was called for a tree with large value of $\rank\br{T}$, the running time increased significantly. Note that this value can be large even for trees with very simple strictures, for example paths are the trees with the largest possible ranking number equal to $\fl{\log\br{n}}+1$. Observing the behaviour of the algorihms manually we noticed that the auxiliary trees for which the QPTAS was called usually had a path-likely structure, which significantly increased the running time of the algorithm. 

The running time of the algorithms measured in the experiments match the theoretical bounds quite closely. The logarithmic plots of the running times of the algorithms which run in polynomial time clearly resemble logarithmic function. Similarly, the plot of the exponential time exact algorithm clearly follows a linear function in the logarithmic scale.

Interestingly, the naive $O\br{n\log n}$ implementation of the linear time algorithm for $T||V||\COST_{max}$ based on calculating the ranking coloring performed differently among the three considered classes of trees. On randomly generated and bounded degree instances the observed running times were almost linear while for the bounded diameter instances the observed running time matches the upper bound of $O\br{n\log n}$. On the other hand for the bounded diameter instances the depth of the decision tree was always at most $4$. This is due to the fact that the depth of the decision tree is trivially upper bounded by $\cl{\frac{\diam\br{T}+1}{2}}$. For randomly generated and bounded degree instances the depth of the decision trees produces by the algorithm behaved logarithmically in $n$. For the latter it is not hard to see that $\rank\br{T} =  \Omega\br{\log_{\Delta}}n$ since each query results in at least one response of size at least $\frac{n-1}{\Delta}$. For random trees the observed logarithmic behavior of $\rank\br{T}$ is more suprising. We conjecture that given a random tree $T$ on $n$ vertices, $\rank\br{T}=\Theta\br{\log n}$. An interesting research direction would be to prove this claim.

The above bound $\rank\br{T}\leq \cl{\frac{\diam\br{T}+1}{2}}$ also shows that for trees with bounded diameter, the running time of the QPTAS for $T||V,c||\COST_{max}$ is in fact polynomial in $n$ and the algorithm becomes a PTAS running in $n^{O\br{\diam\br{T}/\epsilon^2}}$. It would be intersting to verify whether a similar result can be obtained for trees with bounded degree as well. Note that for such trees the ranking is always of order $\Theta\br{\log n}$, so a new technique would have to be designed. However, it is possible that other structural properties of such trees can be exploited to obtain better bounds on the ranking number.

The hardness of constructing extremal instances for the Tree Search Problem is probably the main reason for non existance of hardness results for the problem. This is if further exacerbated by the fact that most classic NP-hard problems are polynomially solvable on trees. All of the NP-hardness results for the problem have relatively simple stricture \cite{Cicalese2012BinIdentPForWTs,Cicalese2016OnTSPwNonUniCost}. It is therefore an interesting research direction to investigate whether it is possible to derive more sophisticated inapproximability results for the problem, tightening the gap between the known approximation algorithms and lower bounds.
